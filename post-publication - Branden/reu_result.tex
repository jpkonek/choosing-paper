\title{Positive-Density Self-Undermining of Risk-Weighted Expected Utility in Finite Dimensions}
\author{}
\date{}
\maketitle

\part{Proof Guide}

\section{The question}

Suppose every individual decision problem is solved using risk-weighted
expected utility (REU). Does choosing the REU-best act in every problem
also maximise REU when all those choices are viewed together as one
higher-order lottery?

The answer is generally no.

The source of the problem is that REU applies a nonlinear transformation
to probabilities. At the individual level, this transformation is applied
inside each decision problem. At the higher level, the same transformation
is applied only after the outcomes from all decision problems have been
aggregated. Since nonlinear transformations do not commute with averaging,
the two optimisation problems can disagree.

\section{Set-up}

Let $\Omega$ be a finite state space, and let $p$ be a full-support
probability measure on $\Omega$. An act is a utility profile
\[
u\in[l,h]^\Omega.
\]

For an act $u$, define its tail probability
\[
H_u(x)
:=
p\bigl(\{\omega\in\Omega:u(\omega)>x\}\bigr).
\]

Let
\[
r:[0,1]\to[0,1]
\]
be strictly increasing, and continuously differentiable, with
\[
r(0)=0,
\qquad
r(1)=1.
\]

The REU value of an act is
\[
\REU_{r,p}(u)
=
l+\int_l^h r(H_u(x))\diff x.
\]

A decision problem is an ordered pair
\[
D=(u,v)\in\D:=([l,h]^\Omega)^2.
\]

A picking strategy $\s$ chooses one of the two acts in every decision
problem:
\[
\s(D)\in\{u,v\}.
\]

Let $\mu$ be a probability distribution over decision problems. We assume
that $\mu$ has a density that is positive almost everywhere on the
interior of $\D$.

The aggregate tail probability generated by $\s$ is
\[
Q_{\s}(x)
:=
\int_{\D} H_{\s(D)}(x)\,\mu(\diff D).
\]

The higher-order REU of $\s$ is
\[
\mathcal V(\s)
:=
l+\int_l^h r(Q_{\s}(x))\diff x.
\]

\section{The result}

\begin{theorem}[Informal self-undermining result]
Suppose that for some non-empty proper event $A\subsetneq\Omega$,
\[
r(p(A))\neq p(A).
\]
Then a strategy that chooses an individually REU-maximising act in every
decision problem need not maximise higher-order REU.

More strongly, every pointwise REU-maximising strategy can be improved by
another strategy that chooses the individually inferior act on a set of
decision problems of positive $\mu$-measure.
\end{theorem}

The remainder of this note explains why.


\section{Infinitesimal contribution to REU of a change in strategy}

The REU of the strategy $\s$ is
\[
\mathcal V(\s)
:=
l+\int_l^h r(Q_{\s}(x))\diff x.
\]
Write
\[
Q(x):=Q_{\s}(x).
\]

Consider the effect of switching from act $a$ to act $b$ in one particular decision problem. 

At each threshold $x$, this switch changes the probability that the
utility produced by the strategy exceeds $x$ by
\[
H_b(x)-H_a(x).
\]

The REU of the strategy weighs the probability of exceeding $x$ by
\[
r(Q(x)).
\]

The measure of this change in strategy will be zero. So it's just an infinitesimal change to $Q$

The sensitivity of this value to a small change in $Q(x)$ is
\[
r'(Q(x)).
\]

Therefore, the marginal contribution of switching from $a$ to $b$ is
\[
\int_l^h
r'(Q(x))
\bigl(H_b(x)-H_a(x)\bigr)
\diff x.
\]

More precisely, imagine making this switch for a very small group of
similar decision problems. If the integral is positive, then making the
switch would infinitesimally improve the REU of the strategy. If it is
negative, then making the switch would infinitesimally reduce the REU of
the strategy.

So, our aim is to find $a$ and $b$ where 
\[ \REU_{r,p}(a)>\REU_{r,p}(b) \]
but where 
\[
\int_l^h
r'(Q(x))
\bigl(H_b(x)-H_a(x)\bigr)
\diff x>0
\]

\section{Constructing such gambles: compare to EU}

Our first attempt will be any two gambles  $a$ and $b$ such that \[ \REU_{r,p}(a)>\REU_{r,p}(b) \] but \[ \EU_p(b)>\EU_p(a). \] 

Such a pair can be obtained from the event $A$ for which \[ r(p(A))\neq p(A). \] 

For example, one may use the constant act and the two-outcome gamble constructed in the Dutch-book argument. Thus REU recommends choosing $a$, whereas EU recommends choosing $b$.

Note that  \begin{equation}\label{eq:1}
\EU_p(b)-\EU_p(a)=\int_l^h (H_b(x)-H_a(x) )\diff x>0
\end{equation}

So, we're close, however, the varying weights $r'(Q(x))$ might reverse the sign.

However, what we can show is that as the difference between $a$ and $b$ become negligible, by a positive affine transformation, $r'(Q(x))$ is more-or-less constant, and positive, meaning that it cannot switch the value. 

\section{Compressing them to find our case}

Fix \(z\in(l,h)\). For \(0<\varepsilon<1\), define
\[
a_\varepsilon
:=
z+\varepsilon(a-z),
\qquad
b_\varepsilon
:=
z+\varepsilon(b-z).
\]
Since \(a,b\in[l,h]^\Omega\), both \(a_\varepsilon\) and
\(b_\varepsilon\) also belong to \([l,h]^\Omega\).

Because REU and EU are invariant under common positive affine
transformations,
\[
\REU_{r,p}(a_\varepsilon)-\REU_{r,p}(b_\varepsilon)
=
\varepsilon
\bigl(
\REU_{r,p}(a)-\REU_{r,p}(b)
\bigr)
>0,
\]
whereas
\[
\EU_p(b_\varepsilon)-\EU_p(a_\varepsilon)
=
\varepsilon
\bigl(
\EU_p(b)-\EU_p(a)
\bigr)
>0.
\]
Thus the REU and EU rankings are preserved under the compression.

We now show that, for sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\int_l^h
r'(Q(x))
\bigl(
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
\bigr)
\diff x
>0.
\]

For any \(x\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
&=
p\bigl(z+\varepsilon(a-z)>x\bigr)\\
&=
p\left(
a>z+\frac{x-z}{\varepsilon}
\right)\\
&=
H_a\left(
z+\frac{x-z}{\varepsilon}
\right),
\end{aligned}
\]
and similarly,
\[
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)
=
H_b\left(
z+\frac{x-z}{\varepsilon}
\right).
\]

The difference
\[
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
\]
vanishes outside the interval
\[
\bigl[
z+\varepsilon(l-z),
z+\varepsilon(h-z)
\bigr].
\]
Using the change of variables
\[
x=z+\varepsilon(y-z),
\qquad
\diff x=\varepsilon\,\diff y,
\]
we obtain
\[
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
\int_l^h
r'(Q(x))
\bigl(
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
\bigr)
\diff x
=
\int_l^h
w\bigl(z+\varepsilon(y-z)\bigr)
\bigl(
H_b(y)-H_a(y)
\bigr)
\diff y.
\]

Since \(w\) is continuous at \(z\),
\[
w\bigl(z+\varepsilon(y-z)\bigr)
\longrightarrow
w(z)
\]
uniformly for \(y\in[l,h]\). It follows that
\[
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
\int_l^h
r'(Q(x))
\bigl(
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
\bigr)
\diff x
\longrightarrow
w(z)
\int_l^h
\bigl(
H_b(y)-H_a(y)
\bigr)
\diff y.
\]

By the tail-integral representation of expected utility,
\[
\int_l^h
\bigl(
H_b(y)-H_a(y)
\bigr)
\diff y
=
\EU_p(b)-\EU_p(a)
>0.
\]
Since \(w(z)>0\), the limit is strictly positive. Hence, for all
sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\int_l^h
r'(Q(x))
\bigl(
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
\bigr)
\diff x
>0.
\]
Thus switching from the individually REU-preferred act
\(a_\varepsilon\) to the individually inferior act
\(b_\varepsilon\) has positive marginal value for the higher-order
objective.


\section{Make an actual change to $\mathcal{V}$}
This has shown an infinitesimal improve in the REU of the strategy by switching to the EU preferred action. To pump it up to an actual improve of the strategy, we just find small balls around our $a$ and $b$ where the same infinitesimal improvement occurs by the switch to the EU preferred option, and then check that this results in a set of decision problems with positive measure, so results in an actual improvement to the REU of the strategy by switching to the EU preferred options.  


\section{What each assumption is doing}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.8cm,style=nextline]
\item[Finite state space]
Makes each act's tail function a finite step function and keeps the
construction transparent.

\item[Full support of $p$]
Ensures that every non-empty proper event has probability strictly between
zero and one.

\item[Positive density of $\mu$]
Ensures that open neighbourhoods of the reversal have positive probability.

\item[Absolute continuity of $\mu$]
Rules out atoms and allows open balls of arbitrarily small positive measure.

\item[Continuity of $r$]
Ensures continuity of individual REU.

\item[Positive derivative of $r$]
Ensures that the higher-order marginal weight $w(x)$ is positive.

\item[Twice differentiability of $r$]
Provides the quadratic Taylor bound needed to control the remainder.

\item[$r(P)\neq P$]
Creates the sign reversal at the centre of the proof.
\end{description}

\section{Conceptual conclusion}

The theorem does not say merely that individual optimisation and social
optimisation can conflict.

The stronger point is that the same REU criterion is used at both levels.
Nevertheless, universal compliance with REU at the individual level is
strictly inferior according to REU applied to the aggregate outcome.

The theory therefore recommends violating its own local recommendations.

\part{Appendix}
\label{sec:pf of lem}Proof of \cref{thm:lem: conv}
\begin{proof}
It follows from a change of variables and the continuity of $w$.

Since
\[
a_\varepsilon=z+\varepsilon a,
\]
we have
\begin{align}
H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
&=
p\bigl(a_\varepsilon>x\bigr)\\
&=
p\bigl(z+\varepsilon a>x\bigr)\\
&=
p\left(
a>\frac{x-z}{\varepsilon}
\right)\\
&=
H_a\left(
\frac{x-z}{\varepsilon}
\right).
\end{align}
Similarly,
\[
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)
=
H_b\left(
\frac{x-z}{\varepsilon}
\right).
\]

Define
\[
I_\varepsilon
:=
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
\int_l^h
w(x)
\bigl(
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
\bigr)
\diff x.
\]

Using the substitution
\[
x=z+\varepsilon y,
\qquad
\diff x=\varepsilon\diff y,
\]
we obtain
\[
I_\varepsilon
=
\int_{(l-z)/\varepsilon}^{(h-z)/\varepsilon}
w(z+\varepsilon y)
\bigl(
H_b(y)-H_a(y)
\bigr)
\diff y.
\]

Because $a$ and $b$ take values in $[l,h]$, their tail functions agree
outside $[l,h]$. Indeed,
\[
H_b(y)-H_a(y)=0
\]
whenever $y<l$ or $y\geq h$.

For sufficiently small $\varepsilon>0$, the interval
\[
\left[
\frac{l-z}{\varepsilon},
\frac{h-z}{\varepsilon}
\right]
\]
contains $[l,h]$. Hence
\[
I_\varepsilon
=
\int_l^h
w(z+\varepsilon y)
\bigl(
H_b(y)-H_a(y)
\bigr)
\diff y.
\]

Since $w$ is continuous at $z$, and $y$ ranges over the bounded interval
$[l,h]$,
\[
w(z+\varepsilon y)\longrightarrow w(z)
\]
uniformly in $y\in[l,h]$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.

Therefore,
\[
I_\varepsilon
\longrightarrow
w(z)
\int_l^h
\bigl(
H_b(y)-H_a(y)
\bigr)
\diff y.
\]

Equivalently,
\[
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
\int_l^h
w(x)
\bigl(
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
\bigr)
\diff x
\longrightarrow
w(z)
\int_l^h
\bigl(
H_b(y)-H_a(y)
\bigr)
\diff y.
\]

Finally, using the tail representation of expected utility,
\[
\int_l^h
\bigl(
H_b(y)-H_a(y)
\bigr)
\diff y
=
\EU_p(b)-\EU_p(a).
\]

Hence
\[
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
\int_l^h
w(x)
\bigl(
H_{b_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{a_\varepsilon}(x)
\bigr)
\diff x
\longrightarrow
w(z)
\bigl(
\EU_p(b)-\EU_p(a)
\bigr).
\]
\end{proof}

\section{Alternative pf}
\begin{proof}
For an act \(u\in[l,h]^\Omega\), write
\[
H_u(x)
:=
p\bigl(\{\omega\in\Omega:u(\omega)>x\}\bigr),
\qquad x\in[l,h],
\]
and put
\[
\Phi(u):=\REU_{r,p}(u).
\]
For the given strategy \(\s\), let
\[
Q(x)
:=
\int_{\Decs}H_{\s(D)}(x)\,\mu(\diff D).
\]
Thus
\[
\REU_{r,p\times \mu}(\s)
=
l+\int_l^h r(Q(x))\,\diff x.
\]

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 1: Local regularity of the aggregate tail function.}

Fix
\[
z\in(l,h),
\]
and identify \(z\) with the constant act
\[
\bar z(\omega)=z
\qquad
\text{for every }\omega\in\Omega.
\]

For each \(\omega\in\Omega\), define
\[
Y_\omega(D):=\s(D)(\omega).
\]
For every \(t\in[l,h]\),
\[
\{D:Y_\omega(D)=t\}
\subseteq
\{(u,v):u(\omega)=t\}
\cup
\{(u,v):v(\omega)=t\}.
\]
The two sets on the right are Lebesgue-null hyperplanes. Since
\(\mu\) is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure,
\[
\mu\{D:Y_\omega(D)=t\}=0.
\]
Thus \(Y_\omega\) has an atomless distribution. It follows that
\[
x\longmapsto \mu\{D:Y_\omega(D)>x\}
\]
is continuous. Since
\[
Q(x)
=
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}
p(\omega)\mu\{D:Y_\omega(D)>x\},
\]
the function \(Q\) is continuous.

Consider the open sets
\begin{align}
B_z^+
&:=
\left\{
(u,v)\in((l,h)^\Omega)^2:
u(\omega)>z,\ v(\omega)>z
\text{ for every }\omega
\right\},\\
B_z^-
&:=
\left\{
(u,v)\in((l,h)^\Omega)^2:
u(\omega)<z,\ v(\omega)<z
\text{ for every }\omega
\right\}.
\end{align}
Both sets are non-empty. Since the density of \(\mu\) is positive
almost everywhere on the interior of \(\Decs\),
\[
\mu(B_z^+)>0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\mu(B_z^-)>0.
\]
On \(B_z^+\), whichever act \(\s\) selects has tail value \(1\) at
\(z\), whereas on \(B_z^-\) it has tail value \(0\). Hence
\[
0<Q(z)<1.
\]

By continuity of \(Q\), there are \(\eta>0\) and \(\kappa>0\) such that
\[
I:=[z-\eta,z+\eta]\subset(l,h)
\]
and
\[
\kappa\leq Q(x)\leq 1-\kappa
\qquad
\text{for every }x\in I.
\]
Define
\[
w(x):=r'(Q(x)),
\qquad x\in I.
\]
Then \(w\) is continuous and strictly positive on \(I\).

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 2: Construct a strict local reversal.}

Let \(A\subsetneq\Omega\) be the non-empty event in the hypothesis, and
write
\[
P:=p(A).
\]
Full support of \(p\) implies
\[
0<P<1.
\]
Since \(r\) is strictly increasing with \(r(0)=0\) and \(r(1)=1\),
we also have
\[
0<r(P)<1.
\]
Set
\[
c:=\frac{P+r(P)}{2}.
\]
Then \(c\in(0,1)\).

For \(\varepsilon>0\), define
\[
g_\varepsilon(\omega)
:=
z+\varepsilon\bigl(\mathbf 1_A(\omega)-c\bigr).
\]
Thus
\[
g_\varepsilon(\omega)
=
\begin{cases}
z+\varepsilon(1-c),&\omega\in A,\\
z-\varepsilon c,&\omega\notin A.
\end{cases}
\]
Take \(\varepsilon>0\) sufficiently small that both values of
\(g_\varepsilon\) lie in the interior of \(I\).

The constant act satisfies
\[
\Phi(\bar z)=z.
\]
Since \(g_\varepsilon\) has lower value \(z-\varepsilon c\), upper
value \(z+\varepsilon(1-c)\), and probability \(P\) of its upper
value,
\begin{align}
\Phi(g_\varepsilon)
&=
l+\int_l^{z-\varepsilon c}1\,\diff x
+\int_{z-\varepsilon c}^{z+\varepsilon(1-c)}
r(P)\,\diff x\\
&=
z+\varepsilon\bigl(r(P)-c\bigr).
\end{align}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
\Phi(g_\varepsilon)-\Phi(\bar z)
=
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigl(r(P)-P\bigr).
\label{eq:individual-comparison}
\end{equation}

Now consider the first-order effect on the higher-order objective:
\[
J_\varepsilon
:=
\int_I
w(x)\bigl(H_{g_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{\bar z}(x)\bigr)
\,\diff x.
\]
Apart from the finitely many endpoints,
\[
H_{g_\varepsilon}(x)-H_{\bar z}(x)
=
\begin{cases}
-(1-P),
&
z-\varepsilon c<x<z,\\
P,
&
z<x<z+\varepsilon(1-c),\\
0,
&
\text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\]
Therefore
\[
J_\varepsilon
=
-(1-P)\int_{z-\varepsilon c}^{z}w(x)\,\diff x
+
P\int_z^{z+\varepsilon(1-c)}w(x)\,\diff x.
\]
Continuity of \(w\) at \(z\) gives
\begin{align}
\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow0}
\frac{J_\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}
&=
w(z)\bigl(-(1-P)c+P(1-c)\bigr)\notag\\
&=
w(z)(P-c)\notag\\
&=
\frac{w(z)}{2}\bigl(P-r(P)\bigr).
\label{eq:higher-order-comparison}
\end{align}
This limit is non-zero. Hence, after decreasing \(\varepsilon\) if
necessary, \(J_\varepsilon\) has the sign of \(P-r(P)\).

Equations
\eqref{eq:individual-comparison} and
\eqref{eq:higher-order-comparison} therefore have opposite signs.
Define an ordered decision problem
\[
D^*:=(u^*,v^*)
\]
by
\[
(u^*,v^*)
:=
\begin{cases}
(\bar z,g_\varepsilon),
&
r(P)<P,\\[3pt]
(g_\varepsilon,\bar z),
&
r(P)>P.
\end{cases}
\]
Then
\begin{equation}
\Phi(u^*)>\Phi(v^*)
\label{eq:strict-individual-ranking}
\end{equation}
but
\begin{equation}
\int_I
w(x)\bigl(H_{v^*}(x)-H_{u^*}(x)\bigr)
\,\diff x
>0.
\label{eq:strict-marginal-ranking}
\end{equation}

Thus \(u^*\) is the unique individually preferred act, while replacing
\(u^*\) by \(v^*\) has a strictly positive first-order effect on the
higher-order objective.

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 3: Thicken the reversal to an open set.}

The map
\[
\Phi:[l,h]^\Omega\longrightarrow\mathbb R
\]
is continuous. Indeed, if \(u_n\to u\), then
\[
H_{u_n}(x)\longrightarrow H_u(x)
\]
for every \(x\) except possibly the finitely many numbers
\[
\{u(\omega):\omega\in\Omega\},
\]
and dominated convergence applies.

For acts \(u,v\) whose coordinates lie in the interior of \(I\), define
\[
G(u,v)
:=
\int_I
w(x)\bigl(H_v(x)-H_u(x)\bigr)
\,\diff x.
\]
This map is continuous. To see this, define
\[
W(y):=\int_z^y w(x)\,\diff x,
\qquad y\in I.
\]
Then
\[
G(u,v)
=
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}
p(\omega)
\bigl(W(v(\omega))-W(u(\omega))\bigr),
\]
which is continuous in \((u,v)\).

By
\eqref{eq:strict-individual-ranking} and
\eqref{eq:strict-marginal-ranking}, there are an open neighbourhood
\(V\) of \(D^*\) and a number \(\gamma>0\) such that, for every
\[
D=(u,v)\in V,
\]
we have
\begin{equation}
\Phi(u)>\Phi(v)
\label{eq:open-individual-ranking}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
G(u,v)>\gamma.
\label{eq:open-marginal-ranking}
\end{equation}
We may shrink \(V\) so that every coordinate of every act appearing
in \(V\) lies in the interior of \(I\).

It follows from \eqref{eq:open-individual-ranking} and the assumed
pointwise optimality of \(\s\) that
\[
\s(u,v)=u
\qquad
\text{for every }(u,v)\in V.
\]

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 4: Switch choices on a small open set.}

Let
\[
M
:=
\sup_{q\in[\kappa/2,\,1-\kappa/2]}
|r''(q)|.
\]
Since \(r\in C^2((0,1))\), we have \(M<\infty\).

Because \(D^*\) lies in the interior of \(\Decs\), there are arbitrarily
small open balls about \(D^*\) contained in \(V\). Every such ball has
strictly positive \(\mu\)-measure, because the density of \(\mu\) is
positive almost everywhere. Moreover, their \(\mu\)-measures converge
to zero as their radii converge to zero, since
\(\mu\ll\operatorname{Leb}\).

We may therefore choose an open ball
\[
\mathcal O\subseteq V
\]
such that, writing
\[
m:=\mu(\mathcal O),
\]
we have
\[
0<m<\frac{\kappa}{2}
\]
and
\begin{equation}
\frac{M}{2}|I|m<\gamma,
\label{eq:small-ball-condition}
\end{equation}
where \(|I|\) denotes the length of \(I\). If \(M=0\), the latter
condition is automatic.

Define a new picking strategy \(\s'\) by
\[
\s'(D)
:=
\begin{cases}
v,
&
D=(u,v)\in\mathcal O,\\
\s(D),
&
D\notin\mathcal O.
\end{cases}
\]
This strategy is measurable. On \(\mathcal O\), it selects the second
act, whereas \(\s\) selects the first.

Define
\[
\delta(x)
:=
\int_{\mathcal O}
\bigl(H_v(x)-H_u(x)\bigr)
\,\mu(\diff(u,v)).
\]
The aggregate tail function induced by \(\s'\) is
\[
Q_{\s'}(x)=Q(x)+\delta(x).
\]
Because every coordinate of every act appearing in \(\mathcal O\)
lies in the interior of \(I\),
\[
\delta(x)=0
\qquad
\text{for }x\notin I.
\]
Also,
\[
|\delta(x)|\leq m
\qquad
\text{for every }x.
\]

By Fubini's theorem and \eqref{eq:open-marginal-ranking},
\begin{align}
\int_I r'(Q(x))\delta(x)\,\diff x
&=
\int_{\mathcal O}
\int_I
w(x)\bigl(H_v(x)-H_u(x)\bigr)
\,\diff x\,
\mu(\diff(u,v))\notag\\
&=
\int_{\mathcal O}G(u,v)\,\mu(\diff(u,v))\notag\\
&\geq
\gamma m.
\label{eq:linear-gain}
\end{align}

For \(x\in I\),
\[
\kappa\leq Q(x)\leq1-\kappa
\]
and
\[
|\delta(x)|\leq m<\frac{\kappa}{2}.
\]
Hence both \(Q(x)\) and \(Q(x)+\delta(x)\), as well as every point
between them, lie in
\[
[\kappa/2,1-\kappa/2].
\]
Taylor's theorem therefore yields
\[
r(Q(x)+\delta(x))-r(Q(x))
\geq
r'(Q(x))\delta(x)
-\frac{M}{2}\delta(x)^2.
\]

Since \(\delta\) vanishes outside \(I\), it follows from
\eqref{eq:linear-gain} that
\begin{align}
\REU_{r,b}(\s')-\REU_{r,b}(\s)
&=
\int_I
\bigl(
r(Q(x)+\delta(x))-r(Q(x))
\bigr)
\,\diff x\\
&\geq
\gamma m
-\frac{M}{2}\int_I\delta(x)^2\,\diff x\\
&\geq
\gamma m
-\frac{M}{2}|I|m^2\\
&=
m\left(
\gamma-\frac{M}{2}|I|m
\right).
\end{align}
By \eqref{eq:small-ball-condition}, the final expression is strictly
positive. Therefore
\[
\REU_{r,b}(\s')>\REU_{r,b}(\s).
\]

Finally, for every \(D=(u,v)\in\mathcal O\),
\[
\Phi(u)>\Phi(v),
\]
so \(v\) is the unique non-\(\REU_{r,p}\)-maximising act in \(D\), and
\(\s'(D)=v\). Consequently,
\[
\mu\left(
\left\{
D\in\Decs:
\s'(D)\notin
\arg\max_{a\in D}\REU_{r,p}(a)
\right\}
\right)
\geq
\mu(\mathcal O)
=
m
>
0.
\]
This proves both conclusions.
\end{proof}

\section{Proof A (using Branden)}

\begin{theorem}[Positive-density self-undermining of risk-weighted expected utility]
\label{thm:reu-positive-density}

Let $\Omega$ be a finite state space, and let $p$ be a probability
function on $\Omega$ such that
\[
p(\omega)>0
\]
for every $\omega\in\Omega$. Fix $l<h$, and identify an act with its
utility profile
\[
u\in[l,h]^\Omega.
\]

Let
\[
\Decs:=([l,h]^\Omega)^2
\]
be the space of ordered binary decision problems. Thus, if
\[
D=(u,v)\in\Decs,
\]
the acts available in $D$ are $u$ and $v$. A picking strategy is a
measurable function
\[
\s:\Decs\longrightarrow[l,h]^\Omega
\]
such that
\[
\s(u,v)\in\{u,v\}
\]
for every $(u,v)\in\Decs$.

Let
\[
r:[0,1]\longrightarrow[0,1]
\]
be continuous and strictly increasing, with
\[
r(0)=0,
\qquad
r(1)=1,
\qquad
r\in C^2((0,1)),
\qquad
r'(x)>0
\]
for every $x\in(0,1)$.

For an act $u$, define
\[
\REU_{r,p}(u)
:=
l+\int_l^h
r\bigl(p(\{\omega\in\Omega:u(\omega)>x\})\bigr)
\,\mathrm{d}x.
\]

Let $\mu$ be a Borel probability measure on $\Decs$. Suppose that
$\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
$\Decs$, and that its density is positive almost everywhere on
\[
((l,h)^\Omega)^2.
\]

Assume complete independence between the state and the decision
problem, so that the joint probability on $\Omega\times\Decs$ is
\[
b=p\times\mu.
\]
For a measurable picking strategy $\s$, define
\[
f_\s(x)
:=
\int_{\Decs}
p\bigl(\{\omega\in\Omega:\s(D)(\omega)>x\}\bigr)
\,\mu(\mathrm{d}D),
\]
and define its higher-order risk-weighted expected utility by
\[
\REU_{r,b}(\s)
:=
l+\int_l^h r(f_\s(x))\,\mathrm{d}x.
\]

Suppose there is a non-empty proper event
\[
A\subsetneq\Omega
\]
such that
\[
r\bigl(p(A)\bigr)\neq p(A).
\]

Then every measurable picking strategy $\s$ satisfying
\[
\s(D)\in
\arg\max_{a\in D}\REU_{r,p}(a)
\]
for every $D\in\Decs$ is strictly suboptimal at the higher-order
level. More precisely, there is a measurable picking strategy $\s'$
such that
\[
\REU_{r,b}(\s')>\REU_{r,b}(\s).
\]

Moreover, $\s'$ can be chosen so that
\[
\mu\left(
\left\{
D\in\Decs:
\s'(D)\notin
\arg\max_{a\in D}\REU_{r,p}(a)
\right\}
\right)>0.
\]
\end{theorem}


\begin{proof}
Write
\[
\mathcal D:=([l,h]^\Omega)^2.
\]
For an act \(u\in[l,h]^\Omega\), define its tail function by
\[
H_u(x)
:=
p\bigl(\{\omega\in\Omega:u(\omega)>x\}\bigr)
=
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}
p(\omega)\mathbf 1_{\{u(\omega)>x\}}.
\]
For a measurable picking strategy \(s\), define
\[
f_s(x)
:=
\int_{\mathcal D}H_{s(D)}(x)\,\mu(\mathrm dD).
\]
Then, by complete independence,
\[
\REU_{r,p\times\mu}(s)
=
l+\int_l^h r(f_s(x))\,\mathrm dx.
\]

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 1: Regularity of the induced tail function.}

For each \(\omega\in\Omega\), the selected utility
\[
D\longmapsto s(D)(\omega)
\]
has an atomless distribution under \(\mu\). Indeed, if
\(N\subseteq[l,h]\) has Lebesgue measure zero, then
\[
\{(u,v):s(u,v)(\omega)\in N\}
\subseteq
\{(u,v):u(\omega)\in N\}
\cup
\{(u,v):v(\omega)\in N\}.
\]
Both sets on the right have \(\mu\)-measure zero because \(\mu\) is
absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. It follows that
\[
x\longmapsto
\mu\{D:s(D)(\omega)>x\}
\]
is continuous for every \(\omega\), and hence \(f_s\) is continuous.

Moreover,
\[
0<f_s(x)<1
\]
for every \(x\in(l,h)\). To see that \(f_s(x)>0\), consider the set
\[
G_x^+
:=
\left\{
(u,v)\in((l,h)^\Omega)^2:
u(\omega)>x
\text{ and }
v(\omega)>x
\text{ for every }\omega\in\Omega
\right\}.
\]
This is a non-empty open subset of the interior of \(\mathcal D\). On
\(G_x^+\), whichever act \(s\) selects has utility greater than \(x\)
in every state, and hence
\[
H_{s(D)}(x)=1.
\]
Since the density of \(\mu\) is positive almost everywhere,
\[
\mu(G_x^+)>0,
\]
and therefore \(f_s(x)>0\).

Similarly, the set
\[
G_x^-
:=
\left\{
(u,v)\in((l,h)^\Omega)^2:
u(\omega)<x
\text{ and }
v(\omega)<x
\text{ for every }\omega\in\Omega
\right\}
\]
is non-empty and open. On this set,
\[
H_{s(D)}(x)=0.
\]
Thus \(\mu(G_x^-)>0\), and consequently \(f_s(x)<1\).

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 2: The shadow value.}

Fix \(y_0\in(l,h)\), and define
\[
W_s(y)
:=
\int_{y_0}^{y}r'(f_s(x))\,\mathrm dx.
\]
Then
\[
W_s'(y)=r'(f_s(y))>0.
\]

Define the shadow value of an act \(u\) by
\[
L_s(u)
:=
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}
p(\omega)W_s(u(\omega)).
\]

The significance of \(L_s\) is that it gives the first-order effect on
the higher-order value of replacing one selected act by another. To
see this, note that
\begin{align}
\int_l^h r'(f_s(x))H_u(x)\,\mathrm dx
&=
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}
p(\omega)
\int_l^{u(\omega)}
r'(f_s(x))\,\mathrm dx
\\
&=
L_s(u)+C_s,
\end{align}
where
\[
C_s
:=
\sum_{\omega\in\Omega}
p(\omega)
\int_l^{y_0}r'(f_s(x))\,\mathrm dx
\]
does not depend on \(u\). Therefore, for any two acts \(u\) and \(v\),
\[
\int_l^h
r'(f_s(x))
\bigl(H_v(x)-H_u(x)\bigr)
\,\mathrm dx
=
L_s(v)-L_s(u).
\]

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 3: A strict reversal between first-order REU and the
shadow value.}

By assumption, there is a non-empty proper event
\[
A\subsetneq\Omega
\]
such that
\[
r(p(A))\neq p(A).
\]
Put
\[
P:=p(A).
\]
Since \(p(\omega)>0\) for every \(\omega\), we have
\[
0<P<1.
\]

Consider the two-parameter family of acts
\[
u^{x,y}(\omega)
:=
\begin{cases}
x, & \omega\in A,\\
y, & \omega\notin A,
\end{cases}
\qquad
l<x,y<h,
\qquad
x>y.
\]
For such an act,
\[
H_{u^{x,y}}(z)
=
\begin{cases}
1, & z<y,\\
P, & y\leq z<x,\\
0, & z\geq x.
\end{cases}
\]
Hence
\[
\REU_{r,p}(u^{x,y})
=
r(P)x+\bigl(1-r(P)\bigr)y.
\]
The common lower-bound term cancels in this expression.

Meanwhile,
\[
L_s(u^{x,y})
=
P W_s(x)+(1-P)W_s(y).
\]

We claim that there are \(x>y\) and \(x'>y'\) such that
\[
\REU_{r,p}(u^{x,y})
>
\REU_{r,p}(u^{x',y'})
\]
but
\[
L_s(u^{x,y})
<
L_s(u^{x',y'}).
\]

Suppose not. Then the gradients of these two functions of \((x,y)\)
must be positively collinear at every point of the open region
\[
\{(x,y)\in(l,h)^2:x>y\}.
\]
Indeed, if the gradients were not positively collinear at some point,
there would be a direction \(d\in\mathbb R^2\) that increased the first
function and decreased the second. A sufficiently small movement in
that direction would produce the required strict reversal.

The two gradients are
\[
\bigl(r(P),1-r(P)\bigr)
\]
and
\[
\bigl(PW_s'(x),(1-P)W_s'(y)\bigr).
\]
Positive collinearity therefore requires
\[
\frac{W_s'(x)}{W_s'(y)}
=
\frac{r(P)(1-P)}
     {(1-r(P))P}
=:C
\]
for every \(x>y\).

Choose
\[
x>y>z.
\]
Then
\[
\frac{W_s'(x)}{W_s'(y)}=C
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{W_s'(y)}{W_s'(z)}=C,
\]
so
\[
\frac{W_s'(x)}{W_s'(z)}=C^2.
\]
But applying the same relation directly to \(x>z\) gives
\[
\frac{W_s'(x)}{W_s'(z)}=C.
\]
Therefore
\[
C^2=C.
\]
Since \(W_s'>0\), we have \(C>0\), and hence
\[
C=1.
\]
It follows that
\[
r(P)(1-P)
=
(1-r(P))P,
\]
and therefore
\[
r(P)=P.
\]
This contradicts
\[
r(p(A))\neq p(A).
\]

Thus there are two acts \(a,c\in((l,h)^\Omega)\) such that
\[
\REU_{r,p}(a)>\REU_{r,p}(c)
\]
but
\[
L_s(c)>L_s(a).
\]

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 4: Thickening the reversal to an open set of decision
problems.}

The two acts \(a\) and \(c\) obtained above are constant on \(A\) and
on its complement. Although such acts lie in a lower-dimensional
subset of the full act space, the two inequalities are strict.
Risk-weighted expected utility is continuous in the utility profile,
and \(L_s\) is continuous because \(W_s\) is continuously
differentiable.

Therefore there are open neighbourhoods
\[
O_a,O_c\subseteq(l,h)^\Omega
\]
of \(a\) and \(c\), respectively, and some \(\eta>0\), such that for
every \(u\in O_a\) and \(v\in O_c\),
\[
\REU_{r,p}(u)>\REU_{r,p}(v)
\]
and
\[
L_s(v)-L_s(u)\geq\eta.
\]

We may shrink \(O_a\) and \(O_c\) so that their closures are compact
subsets of \((l,h)^\Omega\). Thus there is a compact interval
\[
J\subseteq(l,h)
\]
containing every coordinate of every act in
\[
O_a\cup O_c.
\]

Put
\[
N:=O_a\times O_c.
\]
Then \(N\) is a non-empty open subset of the full-dimensional
decision space
\[
((l,h)^\Omega)^2.
\]
Because the density of \(\mu\) is positive almost everywhere,
\[
\mu(N)>0.
\]

For every decision problem
\[
D=(u,v)\in N,
\]
we have
\[
\REU_{r,p}(u)>\REU_{r,p}(v).
\]
Since \(s\) selects an \(\REU_{r,p}\)-maximising act,
\[
s(D)=u
\]
throughout \(N\). However,
\[
L_s(v)-L_s(s(D))
=
L_s(v)-L_s(u)
\geq\eta.
\]

\medskip
\noindent
\textbf{Step 5: A needle variation.}

Since \(\mu\) is absolutely continuous, it is nonatomic. We may
therefore choose a measurable set
\[
E\subseteq N
\]
with arbitrarily small positive measure
\[
\mu(E)=\varepsilon.
\]

Define a new measurable picking strategy \(t\) by
\[
t(D)
:=
\begin{cases}
v, & D=(u,v)\in E,\\
s(D), & D\notin E.
\end{cases}
\]
Thus \(t\) differs from \(s\) only on \(E\), and on \(E\) it selects
the act with the larger shadow value.

Write
\[
g_E(x):=f_t(x)-f_s(x).
\]
Since \(s\) and \(t\) differ only on a set of measure \(\varepsilon\),
and since every tail function takes values in \([0,1]\),
\[
|g_E(x)|\leq\varepsilon
\]
for every \(x\).

Moreover,
\[
g_E(x)=0
\]
outside \(J\). Below the lower endpoint of \(J\), both selected acts
have tail value \(1\); above the upper endpoint of \(J\), both have
tail value \(0\).

Define
\[
B_r(y,z)
:=
r(y)-r(z)-r'(z)(y-z).
\]
Then
\[
r(f_t(x))-r(f_s(x))
=
r'(f_s(x))g_E(x)
+
B_r(f_t(x),f_s(x)).
\]
Therefore
\begin{align}
\REU_{r,p\times\mu}(t)
-
\REU_{r,p\times\mu}(s)
&=
\int_l^h r'(f_s(x))g_E(x)\,\mathrm dx
\\
&\quad+
\int_l^h
B_r(f_t(x),f_s(x))
\,\mathrm dx.
\label{eq:reu-needle-decomposition}
\end{align}

By the definition of \(f_s\) and \(f_t\), and by Fubini's theorem,
\begin{align}
\int_l^h r'(f_s(x))g_E(x)\,\mathrm dx
&=
\int_E
\int_l^h
r'(f_s(x))
\bigl(H_{t(D)}(x)-H_{s(D)}(x)\bigr)
\,\mathrm dx
\,\mu(\mathrm dD)
\\
&=
\int_E
\bigl(L_s(t(D))-L_s(s(D))\bigr)
\,\mu(\mathrm dD).
\end{align}
On \(E\),
\[
L_s(t(D))-L_s(s(D))\geq\eta.
\]
Hence
\[
\int_l^h r'(f_s(x))g_E(x)\,\mathrm dx
\geq
\eta\varepsilon.
\]

It remains to bound the second term in
\eqref{eq:reu-needle-decomposition}. Since \(f_s\) is continuous and
\[
0<f_s(x)<1
\]
for every \(x\in(l,h)\), its image on the compact interval \(J\) lies
in a compact subinterval of \((0,1)\). For sufficiently small
\(\varepsilon\), the same is true of \(f_t\), because
\[
|f_t(x)-f_s(x)|\leq\varepsilon.
\]

Since \(r\in C^2((0,1))\), there is some \(M<\infty\) such that
\[
|r''(z)|\leq M
\]
for every value of \(z\) lying between \(f_s(x)\) and \(f_t(x)\), with
\(x\in J\). Taylor's theorem therefore gives
\[
|B_r(f_t(x),f_s(x))|
\leq
\frac{M}{2}|g_E(x)|^2
\leq
\frac{M}{2}\varepsilon^2.
\]
Because \(g_E\) vanishes outside \(J\),
\[
\left|
\int_l^h
B_r(f_t(x),f_s(x))
\,\mathrm dx
\right|
\leq
\frac{M}{2}\lambda(J)\varepsilon^2,
\]
where \(\lambda(J)\) is the length of \(J\).

Thus, for some constant \(C_0<\infty\),
\[
\REU_{r,p\times\mu}(t)
-
\REU_{r,p\times\mu}(s)
\geq
\eta\varepsilon-C_0\varepsilon^2.
\]
For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\eta\varepsilon-C_0\varepsilon^2>0.
\]
Therefore
\[
\REU_{r,p\times\mu}(t)
>
\REU_{r,p\times\mu}(s).
\]

Finally,
\[
\mu(E)=\varepsilon>0,
\]
and on every \(D\in E\), the strategy \(t\) selects the act \(v\)
even though
\[
\REU_{r,p}(u)>\REU_{r,p}(v).
\]
Thus \(t\) fails to select an \(\REU_{r,p}\)-maximising act on a set
of positive \(\mu\)-measure, as required.
\end{proof}
